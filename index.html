How I got here

I hold a Bachelor's degree in Management from the University of Dhaka, but my design training has come from a different classroom entirely: building and running systems for a live multiplayer game. Since 2022 I've taught myself C#, worked inside the Oxide and Carbon plugin frameworks, and designed the interfaces, progression systems and spatial encounters that players use every day.

That work is sold commercially on the Codefling marketplace — thirty-six published products, priced from $5 to $40 — and built to brief for clients through Fiverr, where I've delivered 279 projects at an average rating of 4.9. Every design decision has had to hold up under real use, not just look right in a mockup.

This portfolio selects ten of those projects, grouped by the design discipline each one belongs to.

01

Interface & information design

Rust's CUI has no layout engine and no real component model: elements are positioned by hand in normalised screen coordinates, and anything beyond a filled rectangle has to be assembled out of image parts. Every interface below is built under those terms.

XP Pro — progression & interface design

InterfaceGame design

Codefling · $25.00 · live · 83 skills across 11 disciplines · 20 permanent bonuses

Rust servers reward grinding, but rarely make a player feel their progress. I designed a full progression system — XP, an 83-skill tree across eleven disciplines, permanent bonuses, prestige tiers and activated abilities — and the interface that makes all of it legible inside a UI system with no layout engine, where every panel is placed by hand.

The hard problem was density. A player at level 19 is holding six statistics, sixteen active bonuses, seven unspent skill points and a tree of eighty-three skills at four different lock states. The entire hierarchy is carried by type weight, a three-step colour code and the rhythm of the grid — there is no component library to fall back on and nothing is responsive, so every one of those alignments is a number I chose.

Abilities are bound to a key and opened as a radial wheel — two levels deep, category first, then the individual ability, with the description resolving in the centre as the selection changes. It's the one place in the plugin that escapes the grid entirely, and it's drawn in a system whose native primitive is a box.

A HUD language for Rust — six shipped interfaces

InterfaceDesign system

Players UI · Minimalist Hud · Vehicle Hud · SimpleStatsUI · Custom Vitals Manager · Toastify — Codefling · free to $15.00

Across six separate products I designed heads-up displays for the same game at six different information densities — from a minimal always-on bar, to a full player panel, to a vehicle-specific readout. Taken together they are one continuous investigation: how much state can sit permanently on screen before it stops being read.

Toastify is the outlier, and the piece I am proudest of. It has no screen of its own — it's a notification primitive that other developers build their own interfaces on top of. Designing a component for people whose use of it you'll never see is a different problem to designing a screen, and it's the closest thing in my practice to design-systems work.

Social Media Rust — an interface moved across media

InterfaceInteractive media

Codefling · $25.00 · live

A working social platform — feed, profiles, posts, comments, bookmarks, trending tags — built to run inside a survival game, on a UI system with no layout engine and no scroll container you get for free. Every convention the form depends on had to be rebuilt by hand out of primitives the game never intended for it.

What shipped keeps most of the form intact: an authored feed, profiles with a banner, an avatar and follower, like and post counts, an About Me, image posts, likes, bookmarks and threaded comments, plus a search field, a trending-hashtag board and a most-liked-users ranking down the right-hand side. Chat, notifications and a follow list sit in a persistent left rail.

What had to change are the parts that depend on scale. Trending hashtags on a server of a hundred players surface at one post each, and a most-liked table ranks a handful of names rather than millions, so the whole thing reads as a small town rather than a network. The content is the game itself — the screenshots are raids, the captions are threats — which turns a format built for broadcasting into one for taunting people you will meet again within the hour.

SimplePVE — an authoring tool for 55 rules

InterfaceSystems

Codefling · $19.99 · live · 55 rules · 7 categories · scheduled profiles

A server's combat rules are 55 separate switches. Left as a config file they're unreadable and nobody touches them; the design problem is turning that many booleans into something an owner can actually hold in their head and change with confidence while players are online.

My answer was to stop treating them as a list. Rules are grouped into seven categories and edited one category at a time; complete sets are saved as named profiles — a Full PVE weekday rule set, a Weekend Raid one — which can be duplicated, previewed, scheduled and set live as a unit. The owner reasons about two named states, not fifty-five switches.

Every destructive control is colour-coded and separated from the safe ones, because the whole panel edits a live server. Benchmarked for performance and patched for compatibility with other plugins that change the same state underneath it.

Encounter & time-based design

Events that unfold over time on a live server: paced sequences, adapted formats, and systems that hand authorship back to players.

Arc Launch Site & Monument Events — paced encounters

Time-basedGame design

Codefling · $25.00 and $19.99 · live · related: Military Airfield Event $14.99

Static loot events don't hold attention. I designed a drone-piloted launch-site sequence — writing a custom flight controller so the drone reads as autonomous rather than scripted — and NPC-populated monument encounters with spawn and navigation logic written from the ground up. Both run as recurring live events.

The design work here is pacing, not content: how long the approach takes, how much warning players get, and how wide the window is between arrival and resolution.

Glass Bridge & Floor Drops — adapting a format

Time-basedGame design

Codefling · $14.99 each · live

Two events that take a structure from outside games — an elimination format built on forced choice under time pressure — and rebuild it as playable rules inside a survival server.

They answer it two different ways. Glass Bridge makes every step a forced choice with nothing behind it to reason from, so the pressure is a decision. Floor Drops takes the ground away round by round, so the pressure is the space. One eliminates by guess, the other by geometry — and both have to hold up in front of an audience who can shoot back, which is the part no televised version ever has to solve.

03

Spatial design

Designing navigable space — and drawing it.

Maze — designed space

SpatialGame design

Codefling · $40.00 · my highest-priced product · alongside: Maze Spawnable $25.00

A navigable maze that drops into a live server world and runs as a timed elimination event. Unlike everything else in this portfolio the material here is space itself: sightlines, dead ends, the rate at which a player loses their bearings, and where they are allowed to recover them.

The event frames the space rather than the other way round. A countdown holds players outside a sealed entrance, the doors open, and a second clock starts closing them — so the maze has to be readable enough to enter under pressure and confusing enough to be worth entering at all. Wall tops are wired, which removes climbing as an answer and forces every route decision back down to the floor.

Web design & development

Design carried out of the game and onto the open web — one built to an existing specification, one authored end to end.

IRANRUST — designing in Persian, right to left

Web designDevelopment

ir-rust.ir · live · Persian (Farsi), fully right-to-left · 3 servers, 2,656+ registered players

A full community platform for one of Iran's largest Rust communities, designed and built entirely in Persian and entirely right-to-left. Almost every layout assumption inverts: reading order, alignment, the side a label sits on, the direction a bar fills, where the most significant digit of a countdown belongs.

It also runs on a different calendar. Dates are Jalali rather than Gregorian, so the news feed and the wipe schedule had to be built around a system whose months are different lengths and whose current year is 1404.

Past the marketing pages it carries live status for three servers with capacity and time-to-wipe, a leaderboard across 29 statistical categories refreshing every five minutes, and a team-finding board where players post as looking-for-team or looking-for-players — with a chat that only opens once both sides have accepted.

Two more server sites — rustova.net & Sweden Rust

Web designDevelopment

rustova.net — PHP, built end to end, my own server · swedenrust.com — Nordic community site

rustova.net is my own server's site, built in PHP end to end: store, leaderboard, ban list, support and admin tools. Every layout, content and information-architecture decision on it is mine, with no client and no brief — the only project here where I was the audience as well as the designer.

Sweden Rust is the same problem for someone else's community, answered differently. Its home page is built around time: a live player count, running countdowns to the helicopter, cargo ship, Bradley and deep-sea events, and a schedule of community events with their prizes. The page is worth reloading because the numbers move.

Three server sites for three audiences — my own, a Persian community, a Nordic one — make a useful comparison: the same underlying content reorganised three times around who is reading it.

Browser tools for the people who run my plugins

Web designTool design

xppro-editor.vercel.app & npcbands.vercel.app · live, free to use

Both of these exist because of a complaint. My plugins are configured by hand-editing JSON, and for anything the size of XP Pro that is genuinely miserable — one missing brace and a server owner has a broken server and no idea why.

XPPro Editor takes the plugin's own data files — the progression ladder, the buffs, the 83 skills — and turns editing them into a visual task. You drag LevelsData.json onto the page, change what you need across three linked editors, and download a file that is valid by construction. Nothing is uploaded; it all runs in the browser.

NPC Bands Track Generator is stranger, and I like it more. The plugin puts NPC musicians in the world; the generator takes a MIDI file, converts it into a track the in-game instruments can actually play, and hands it back. It turns “write music for Rust” into “drop in a MIDI file”.

These are the only things here designed for an audience that is neither a player nor a client, but someone trying to operate what I made. Different failure states, less tolerance for ambiguity, and no tutorial.

Designing to a brief, 279 times

Alongside the products above, I've delivered 279 commissioned projects through Fiverr at an average rating of 4.9 — 189 of them on a single service. Clients arrive with a list, not a specification. Turning one into the other is the design work.

01
Brief
The client's own words — usually a feature list, often contradictory, rarely prioritised.
02
Interpretation
What they asked for versus what they want. Resolving conflicts, naming what's missing, proposing what they didn't know to ask for.
03
Build
Configuration, interface design and custom work against the agreed specification.
04
Live
Handover to a running server with real players, then iteration against what actually happens.
“5x Gathers and Loots, Raid basses spawn on the map, A level system like chopping wood and much other activity, Loot tables to be accurate to 5x or a little higher, Custom component loot, Better Airdrops, Better Helicopters, Monuments to have better Loot, Stacks to be well made, Kits for players and Donated players, Shop setup in-game, Welcome panel setup with good design, Fast Crafting times, Make it so everyone has Tier 1 workbench fully learned, Smelting times to 5x speed, Clans setup, Custom chat tags for donors and Stuff members, And a Tebex website setup with automatic rank given to players upon purchases.” — client brief, Krom Romania EU 5x, quoted verbatim. Delivered in full.

Twenty instructions, and exactly one of them is about design: “welcome panel setup with good design”. Everything else is a quantity, a rate or a switch — verifiable, and largely a configuration task. The one line carrying any judgement is also the only one with no way to check it, and it is the line the client will actually remember.

That asymmetry is the job. Most of a brief like this can be delivered exactly as written; the value sits in the single item that cannot be, and in noticing which item that is before starting.
